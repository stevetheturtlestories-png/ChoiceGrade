How to compare HVAC replacement quotes

A new furnace, air conditioner or heat pump is more than a box with a price tag. This guide helps you compare what each installer will supply, change, test and stand behind.

Compare the quotesCopy the questions
Five-minute approach

Put every quote side by side. Mark each item included, excluded or unclear. Ask the same written questions of each contractor, then compare their revised offers. An unanswered item is a question, not proof of poor workmanship.

First: are they quoting the same job?

Tell each installer the same goals: which rooms are uncomfortable, whether you want cooling, your interest in a heat pump, how long you plan to stay, and any electrical or duct problems you know about. Request an itemized written scope, including exact model numbers and what stays in place.

A three-ton label on your old unit is not a heat-loss calculation for your current home. Insulation, windows, air leakage and duct conditions can change the right equipment choice. ENERGY STAR advises a home evaluation and checking ductwork and airflow.

What “same price” can conceal

Bid A replaces the outdoor heat pump and indoor coil. Bid B also includes an electrical circuit, a thermostat that controls backup heat, and duct modifications. The bids cannot be compared fairly until you ask A to include or explicitly exclude those items.

Illustrative example; no claim about typical prices or any particular installer.

Nine details to compare line by line

ItemLook forAsk this
1. SizingHeating and cooling load calculation; proposed capacity; how existing comfort problems are addressed.“What calculation supports this size, and can I see the summary?”
2. Equipment matchOutdoor unit, indoor coil or air handler, furnace if applicable, exact model numbers, and rated combination.“Can you list every model number and the AHRI reference or equivalent rating information?”
3. Cold-weather planFor heat pumps: heating capacity at relevant outdoor temperatures, backup heat type and control settings.“At what conditions will backup run, and what happens in our coldest weather?”
4. Air deliveryExisting duct evaluation, filter and return-air path, needed transitions, sealing or repairs.“Will the present ducts deliver the required airflow? What duct work is included?”
5. Installation scopeRefrigerant line set, condensate drain, outdoor location, electrical work, venting and disposal as applicable.“What will be reused? What is excluded and could cost extra?”
6. ControlsThermostat compatibility, zoning if proposed, setup, and demonstration to the homeowner.“Will you set up and show me the heating, cooling and backup settings?”
7. Handover testsManufacturer-recommended refrigerant charge checks, airflow verification, safety and startup checks; written results.“Which readings will you record and leave with me?”
8. Money and timingPermits, required inspections, start date, change-order approval, payment schedule, rebates if applicable.“Which costs are fixed, and what requires my written approval?”
9. WarrantyManufacturer parts, installer labor, registration responsibilities, exclusions and service contact.“Who pays for labor if a covered part fails, and for how long?”

For a furnace-only or AC-only job, mark heat-pump-specific items “not applicable.” Rules and permits vary by location; confirm locally.

Why the details matter

Sizing and ducts: Oversizing can lead to short cycling and comfort problems. A well-sized unit still needs adequate airflow and suitable ducts. Ask for a load-calculation summary and a written plan for any duct issues. ENERGY STAR’s own bid comparison checklist asks about Manual J or equivalent, duct condition, airflow and testing.

Matched equipment: An efficiency number may describe a specific outdoor and indoor combination, not every possible mix. Ask for exact models and, where applicable, an AHRI certificate or reference number. AHRI explains that its certificate lists the models and certified ratings.

Cold-climate heat pumps: A unit’s advertised ability to operate at a low temperature does not by itself tell you how much heat it delivers there. Ask the installer to compare the home’s heating load with the quoted unit’s capacity at your local design temperature and specify when backup heat operates. Gas backup, electric resistance backup and no backup are different projects.

Commissioning: Ask what the crew will measure after installation, not merely whether the unit switches on. ENERGY STAR’s installation guidance discusses verifying airflow, refrigerant charge, and furnace or boiler operation as applicable.

Two situations worth catching before signing

“Our old unit was three tons, so we quoted three tons”

That may turn out to be the correct size. Ask each bidder what measurements and load assumptions support it. If one proposal includes duct changes and the other assumes the existing duct can handle the equipment, request the duct findings in writing.

“This heat pump works down to −25°C”

That single number does not answer how your home is heated on a cold day. Ask for the unit’s heating capacity at a relevant outdoor temperature, an estimated home heat load, and the backup-heat sequence. Also ask whether electrical upgrades are in the quoted price.

These are illustrative scenarios, not accounts of specific customers or promises about a particular product.

Email the same questions to every bidder

Subject: Clarifications on my HVAC replacement quote

Hi [name], thanks for your proposal. Before I decide, could you please confirm:

  1. The exact model numbers of all included equipment and the AHRI certificate or rating documentation if applicable.
  2. What load calculation supports the proposed size, and whether you checked the existing ductwork and airflow.
  3. What work is included for ducts, refrigerant lines, electrical, venting, drains, controls, permits and disposal; and what is excluded.
  4. If this is a heat pump, its heating plan for our coldest weather, including backup heat and controls.
  5. Which startup measurements and documents you will leave with me after installation.
  6. The manufacturer parts warranty, your labor warranty, and the process for service calls.
  7. Any possible extra costs, how changes are approved, and the expected start and finish dates.

Could you include these answers in the written proposal? Thank you, [your name]

Before you choose

  • Compare the revised scope after receiving answers, not only the first totals.
  • Verify the business, references, license or certification and insurance where required locally.
  • Put the final models, scope, payment terms and warranty responsibilities in the signed agreement.
  • Keep your quote, change orders, permit and inspection records, test results, manuals and warranty paperwork.
